# thinga — 2026-09-11

These are learner corrections from the lesson with John. There are 10 items about meaning, terms, register and false friends.

### 1. "Intelligence" instead of level or seniority
**You:** "we got a new employee that got quite the same, that was the same intelligence as me."
**Better:** "we got a new employee that was at the same level as me."
`the same intelligence → at the same level`

> *Intelligence* means how smart someone is. The interviewer hears "he was as clever as me," which sounds odd and says nothing about his experience or seniority.

### 2. "Prior" used to mean "main"
**You:** "Since the person was qualified enough, the prior thing I did was during the code review."
**Better:** "Since the person was qualified enough, the main thing I did was during the code review."
`prior → main`

> *Prior* means "earlier" or "before," so this sounds like you did something before the code review. What you meant was that code review was your main tool.

### 3. "Put off" instead of "leave out"
**You:** "We're usually trying to put off a specific tool or thing."
**Better:** "We're usually trying to leave out a specific tool or thing."
`put off → leave out`

> *Put off* means postpone ("I put off the meeting") or discourage ("the price put me off"). You meant "not mention," and that is *leave out*.

### 4. "Stakeholder" used for a new hire
**You:** "Usually the team wants that the new stakeholder."
**Better:** "Usually the team wants that the new team member."
`stakeholder → team member`

> A stakeholder is someone with an interest in the project's outcome, such as business owners, clients or managers. A developer you are onboarding is a *team member* or a *new hire*.

### 5. "Crucial" instead of "critical" for bug severity
**You:** "Sometimes it's crucial enough to roll back."
**Better:** "Sometimes it's critical enough to roll back."
`crucial → critical`

> Bug severity is described as *critical*, *major* or *minor*. *Crucial* means "decisive, key to success," so an interviewer would not expect it when you are talking about how bad a bug is.

### 6. "Fair enough" used to mean "reasonable"
**You:** "In this case, it's probably fair enough to roll back and fix everything at the first step."
**Better:** "In this case, it probably makes sense to roll back and fix everything at the first step."
`it's probably fair enough → it probably makes sense`

> *Fair enough* is a reply that accepts someone else's point ("Fair enough, let's do that."). Inside your own argument it sounds like you are giving in, not recommending a decision.

### 7. "Lower tasks" instead of "smaller tasks"
**You:** "And split it for some lower tasks."
**Better:** "And split it into some smaller tasks."
`for → into` · `lower → smaller`

> In Jira, *lower* sounds like lower priority. The usual phrase for breaking down an epic is *split it into smaller tasks*.

### 8. "Commercial experience" can say you've never had a paid job
**You:** "Because I don't have a commercial experience, but I have a bunch of uncommercial ones."
**Better:** "Because I don't have this experience at work, but I have a bunch of it outside of work."
`a commercial experience → this experience at work` · `uncommercial ones → it outside of work`

> In an interview, "I don't have commercial experience" means "I have never worked as a paid developer," which isn't true for you. *Uncommercial* is not a word. *Non-commercial* means "not-for-profit."

### 9. "Translated" instead of "transcribed"
**You:** "So all of my answers will be recorded and translated to script directly."
**Better:** "So all of my answers will be recorded and transcribed directly."
`translated to script → transcribed`

> *Translate* always means into another language, so John would picture your answers being turned into Russian. Turning speech into text is *transcribe*, and the result is a *transcript*.

### 10. "Shortly speaking" is a calque of *короче говоря*
**You:** "Shortly speaking, I have wrote in around 5,000 lines of code for every day."
**Better:** "In short, I have written around 5,000 lines of code every day."
`Shortly speaking → In short` · `for every day → every day`

> *Shortly speaking* isn't an English phrase. Native speakers say *In short*, *Long story short* or *Basically*. You start with this phrase often, so fixing it is worth it.
